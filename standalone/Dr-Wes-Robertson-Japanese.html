Giving & Receiving in Japanese

Why this matters: In English, "give" and "receive" are simple — you give to someone, you receive from someone. In Japanese, the choice of verb depends on who is doing the giving relative to your in‑group. This reflects the strong emphasis on in‑group/out‑group relationships in Japanese culture. Getting this wrong can sound rude or confusing, so it's worth understanding the logic. Key insight: Think of inward (toward you or your group) vs. outward (away from you or your group). The verb you choose tells the listener the direction of the giving.

Japanese has three different verbs for "to give."
Your choice depends on who is giving what to whom.

The key factor is whether the gift moves:

  • Inward — from outside your group to inside your group
  • Outward — from inside your group to outside your group

In‑Group / Out‑Group Examples

SituationDirection
Your dad gives something to you✅ Inward
You give something to your dad❌ Outward
Your boss gives something to your dad✅ Inward (dad is closer to you than your boss)
Someone from another company gives to your boss✅ Inward (your company is your in‑group)
Your dad gives something to your boss❌ Outward
Your boss gives to another company❌ Outward

1. くれる — Inward Giving

When to use くれる: Use it when someone gives something to you or someone in your in‑group. The "giver" is marked by は or が, and the "receiver" (who is in your in‑group) is marked by に. You never use くれる when you or someone in your group is the giver — that would be outward.

Use くれる when something moves inward.
The recipient is marked by に.

父ちちが私わたしに本ほんをくれました。 My father gave (inwards) a book to me.
父パパに何なにをくれた？ What did you (outside our family) give my dad?

2. あげる — Outward Giving

When to use あげる: Use it when you or someone in your in‑group gives something to someone outside. The giver is marked by は or が, and the recipient is marked by に. Be careful: using あげる to talk about your own actions can sound pretentious — like you're doing someone a big favor. In casual situations, Japanese people often avoid あげる when talking about their own giving, especially to close friends or family.

Use あげる when something moves outward.
The recipient is marked by に.

私わたしは父ちちに本ほんをあげた。 I gave (outwards) a book to my dad.

3. やる — Outward (disrespectful / casual)

When to use やる: This is also outward giving, but it carries a tone of looking down on the recipient or is used for non‑human things (animals, plants). Use it when you're giving something to someone you consider beneath you, or to a pet/plant. It can sound aggressive if used with people, so be careful. In modern Japanese, やる is often replaced with あげる even for animals to sound more polite.
野良猫のらねこに餌えさを買かってやった。 I bought the stray cat food (casual, non‑human).
俺おまえに教おしえてやる。 I'll teach you a lesson (you scum).

4. もらう — Receiving

When to use もらう: This is the "receiving" verb — it doesn't care about direction, only that you received something. The giver is marked by に or から, and the receiver (you or your in‑group) is marked by は or が. This is the safest verb to use when you're the receiver, because it doesn't involve the complex in‑group/out‑group calculations of the giving verbs.

もらう means "to receive".
The giver is marked by に or から.
The receiver is marked by は / が.

父ちちから本ほんをもらった。 I got a book from my dad.

5. Verb + て Form + Giving Verbs

Why this matters: Attaching くれる, あげる, or もらう to the て‑form of another verb turns the action into a favor. It indicates that the action was done for someone's benefit. The same in‑group/out‑group rules apply — くれる means the favor was done for you or your group, あげる means you did a favor for someone outside.
JapaneseMeaning
父ちちに作つくってくれた。Someone outside made it for my dad (inward).
父ちちに作つくってあげた。I made it for my dad (outward).
説明せつめいしてくれる？Will you explain it to me (as a favor)?

6. Side‑by‑Side Comparison

See the difference: These four sentences all involve a book being transferred between Tanaka and me. The verb choice completely changes who is doing what, and whether Tanaka is in‑group or out‑group. Pay attention to the particles — they change to match the verb's direction.
たなかさんは私わたしに本ほんをくれました。 Tanaka gave (inwards) a book to me.
私わたしはたなかさんに本ほんをあげました。 I gave (outwards) a book to Tanaka.
私わたしはたなかさんに本ほんをもらいました。 I received a book from Tanaka.

Things You Can Do With ~て Form

What is the て‑form? The て‑form (also called the "te‑form") is one of the most versatile verb forms in Japanese. It's like a verb‑connector — it lets you attach other verbs to change the meaning. Think of it as the "and then" or "in order to" form. Once you know the て‑form of a verb, you can make many different expressions by attaching different helper verbs. This section covers the three most common uses.

1. 〜てみる — Try doing (for the first time)

What it means: 〜てみる means "to try doing [verb] for the first time" or "to give [verb] a shot". It implies that you're doing something new — you've never done it before, or at least you haven't done it in this particular context. Important: It does NOT mean "try hard" or "make an effort" — that's a different expression (〜しようとしている). Use 〜てみる when you're experimenting or testing something.
Dictionary飲のむ
→
て form飲のんで
→
〜てみる飲のんでみる
Polite: 飲のんでみます。
先生せんせいにきいてみるね！ I'll try asking the teacher.
納豆なっとうをたべてみましたか。 Did you try eating natto (for the first time)?
⚠️ 〜てみる cannot mean "try" as in "making efforts".
日本語にほんごをべんきょうしようとしている。 — I'm making efforts to study Japanese. (This means you're struggling/working hard at it, not just trying it for the first time.)

2. 〜ておく — Do in advance / in preparation

What it means: 〜ておく means "to do [verb] in advance" or "to do [verb] as preparation". It implies that you're doing something now so that you're ready for something later. It's like "let me get this done ahead of time." The おく is the verb meaning "to put/place" — so literally, you're "putting" the action in place for later.
Dictionary飲のむ
→
て form飲のんで
→
〜ておく飲のんでおく
Polite: 飲のんでおきます。
服ふくを洗あらっておいた。 I washed the clothes in preparation.
明日あしたのランチをつくっておくね。 I'll get tomorrow's lunch ready early, okay?

3. 〜てほしい — Want something done (by someone)

What it means: 〜てほしい means "I want [someone] to do [verb]". It expresses a desire for someone else to take an action. The person you want to do the action is marked by に. Be careful: This can sound demanding or childish if used with superiors or in formal situations. It's fine with friends, family, or people below you, but for polite requests, use 〜てもらえませんか instead.
⚠️ Avoid this when requesting from superiors. Use 〜もらえませんか for polite requests.

Polite: 飲のんでほしいです。

4. 〜ている — Progressive / Result / Habitual

What it means: 〜ている is one of the most important verb forms in Japanese. It has three main uses:
  1. Progressive: An action is happening right now ("I am eating").
  2. Resultant state: A completed action has left a lasting result ("I am married" — the action of marrying happened, and the state continues).
  3. Habitual: Something done regularly ("I read the newspaper every morning").
Context is key to understanding which meaning is intended. If it's an action that takes time (like 食べる — eating), it's usually progressive. If it's a change of state (like 結婚する — to get married), it's usually resultant state.
今いま、飯めしを食たべている。 I am eating right now. (progressive)
結婚けっこんしている。 I am married. (resultant state)

Passive / Causative / Causative‑Passive

Three related forms: These three forms are built from each other and are used to shift who is doing the action and who is affected.
  • Passive: "I was verbed by someone." (The focus is on the receiver.)
  • Causative: "I made/let someone verb." (The focus is on the causer.)
  • Causative‑Passive: "I was made to verb by someone." (The focus is on the person being forced.)
Think of it as a chain of responsibility: who is doing the action, and who is affected by it?

1. Passive Form

What it does: The passive form shifts the focus from the doer to the receiver. In Japanese, it's used much more often than in English — not just when someone "was verbed," but also to avoid directly blaming someone (since Japanese culture values indirectness) and to show respect (the honorific passive). The "doer" is marked by に, and the "receiver" (the topic) is marked by は.

2. Causative Form — make/let

What it does: The causative form means "to make/let someone do [verb]". The person doing the causing (the "causer") is marked by は, and the person being caused (the "doer") is marked by に. The difference between "make" and "let" is determined by context — not by the verb form itself. Words like むりやり (against will) or すきなだけ (to one's heart's content) clarify the meaning.

3. Causative‑Passive — was made to do

What it does: The causative‑passive form means "was made/caused to do [verb] by someone". It combines the causative (making someone do something) with the passive (the focus is on the receiver). The person being forced is marked by は, and the person doing the forcing is marked by に. This is often used to complain about being forced to do something!

する → させられる  |  くる → こさせられる

Comparison

Notice the particle changes: In the passive, は marks the receiver (the person affected) and に marks the doer. In the causative, は marks the causer and に marks the doer. In the causative‑passive, は marks the person being forced and に marks the person doing the forcing. The particles tell you who is responsible and who is affected.
私わたしはサメを食たべる。 I eat shark.
私わたしはサメに食たべられる。 I will be eaten by a shark. (passive)
私わたしはサメに食たべさせる。 I let/make the shark eat. (causative)
私わたしはサメに食たべさせられる。 I will be forced to eat by a shark. (causative-passive)

Volitional & Potential Forms

1. Volitional — "Let's ~" / "Shall we ~"

What it does: The volitional form means "let's do [verb]" or "I will do [verb]" (with a sense of intention). It's used to suggest doing something together or to state your own intention. The polite version is 〜ましょう — this is the form you learn first in textbooks. The plain volitional is used in casual conversation. When combined with 〜としている, it means "trying to do" (making an effort). When combined with 〜と思っている, it means "planning to do."

Volitional + 〜としている — making efforts

What this means: This expresses that someone is making an effort to do something — they're actively working toward the goal, but may not have achieved it yet. It's different from 〜てみる (trying for the first time) — this is about struggling or working hard at something.
たばこをやめようとしている。 I'm trying to quit smoking. (making an effort, possibly struggling)

Volitional + 〜と思っている — planning

What this means: This expresses that someone is planning to do something — they have made a decision and intend to carry it out. It's stronger than 〜たい (I want to do) and implies more commitment.
帰かえろうと思っている。 I'm thinking of going home. (planning/deciding to)

2. Potential — can(not) do

What it does: The potential form expresses ability — "can do" or "cannot do" something. It's a way to talk about your skills, capabilities, or permissions. Notice that the formal grammar rule says to use が instead of を for the object of a potential verb — but in modern Japanese, many people use を anyway. ら抜き言葉 (dropping the ら from 〜られる) is common in casual speech, but some consider it grammatically incorrect.

Irregular: する → できる  |  くる → こられる

る Verbs — replace る with 〜られる

あした食たべられるよ。 I can eat tomorrow.

ビールが飲のめる。 I can drink beer. (always correct with が)
⚠️ ら抜き言葉: 食べられる → 食べれる (shortened, unambiguous potential. Some consider it informal.)

The ~たい Form — "want to [verb]"

The logic of 〜たい: In Japanese, expressing desire is more complex than in English. The 〜たい form attaches to the verb stem and means "I want to do [verb]." However, you cannot use 〜たい to directly describe other people's desires — this would be like you're claiming to know what's inside someone else's head, which is considered presumptuous. Instead, you use other forms like 〜たがっている (observable behavior) or 〜たいそうです (hearsay) or quote them directly. Notice also that the object of a 〜たい verb can be marked by either を or が — both are acceptable, but が is more common.

1. Basic Form

Attach 〜たい to the verb stem (〜ます form without ます).

2. Particle Usage: を vs. が

私わたしはりんごを食たべたいです。 I want to eat an apple. (using を)
私わたしはりんごが食たべたいです。 I want to eat an apple. (using が — also common)
⚠️ Particles other than を never change to が. (e.g., と stays と, に stays に)

3. Restrictions — When NOT to use 〜たい

〜たい is not acceptable for describing other people's desires. Use the alternatives below.
Why the restriction? In Japanese culture, it's considered rude or presumptuous to claim you know what someone else wants. By using 〜たい for someone else, you're making a statement about their inner state that you can't actually verify. Instead, Japanese has several ways to indirectly describe others' desires:
  • 〜たがっている — based on observable behavior ("they seem to want")
  • 〜たいそうです — hearsay ("I heard they want")
  • 〜たいと言っている — direct quotation ("they said they want")
1 Your own desires — ✅ Always acceptable 私わたしは日本にほんに行いきたいです。 I want to go to Japan.
2 Asking questions — ✅ Acceptable but can be rude (in any language!) 日本にほんでなにをやりたい？ What do you want to do in Japan?
3 General desires — ✅ Acceptable when not specific to an individual ビールを飲のみたい人ひとは手てをあげてください。 Everyone who wants to drink beer, raise your hands.

4. Describing Others' Desires

〜たがっている — observable behavior

Use this when the person's behavior suggests they want to do something — they're looking at it, reaching for it, etc. It's a safe way to talk about others' desires without claiming to know their thoughts.
その犬いぬは晩御飯ばんごはんを食たべたがっている。 That dog (is behaving as though it) wants to eat dinner.
⚠️ 〜たがっている is a verb — do NOT change を to が.

〜たいそうです — hearsay

Use this when you heard someone say they want to do something. It's a report of what you were told, not a claim about their inner state.
あまりやりたくないそうです。 From what I gather, they don't really want to do it. (hearsay)

〜たいと言っている — quoting

Use this to directly quote someone stating their own desire. This is the most explicit and accurate way to describe someone else's wants.
かれは日本語にほんごをべんきょうしたいと言っていた。 He was saying that he wants to study Japanese.

Basic Verb Conjugation in Japanese

The big picture: Japanese verbs are surprisingly logical once you understand the patterns. There are only three verb types:
  • Irregular verbs (する and くる) — only two! You just memorize them.
  • る verbs (ichidan) — verbs ending in 〜る that are regular. The る is part of the stem.
  • う verbs (godan) — most verbs. The final sound changes depending on the conjugation.
The key to mastering Japanese verbs is recognizing which type you're dealing with. The 〜ます form (polite) makes everything regular — all verbs conjugate the same way in polite speech. The difficulty comes with the plain form, where each verb type behaves differently.

1. Polite Form (〜ます)

The easy one: In polite form, all verbs conjugate the same way. Present positive is 〜ます, negative is 〜ません, past is 〜ました, past negative is 〜ませんでした. The 〜ます ending can mean either present tense (I do) or future tense (I will do) — context tells you which. This is why you learn 〜ます form first in textbooks — it's predictable and polite.
Positive食たべますI (will) eat.
Negative食たべませんI do/will not eat.
Past食たべましたI ate.
Past Neg.食たべませんでしたI did not eat.

2. Plain Form

The tricky one: In plain form, each verb type follows its own pattern. But the good news is that once you know the plain form, you can derive everything else. Irregulars (する, くる) just need to be memorized. る verbs replace る with the new ending. う verbs shift the final sound — [u] becomes [a] for negatives, [e] for potential, [o] for volitional, etc. The pattern is consistent across all う verbs.
